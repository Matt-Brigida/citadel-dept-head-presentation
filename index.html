[2:45 to 4:45 | 2 minutes] Shared governance is not a brake on leadership. It is how decisions become durable. Two examples are underway in my department right now. First, the accounting faculty are reworking our undergraduate curriculum for the CPA-120 pathway. The design belongs to them; they own the expertise and they will teach it. My job is to frame the constraints honestly, put the evidence on the table, and carry their conclusion forward with real advocacy. Second, the faculty are deciding to retire a concentration in our Master's in Accounting. Making that decision workable is my part: I am working with our junior faculty to find alternative online courses, so the students are served and those colleagues can keep their time for research. There is a corollary. Some decisions are made above the department, by the dean or the provost. When that is the case, I say so plainly rather than stage a consultation whose outcome is already fixed; faculty can tell the difference. My job then is to bring the department's view upward with real advocacy, and to report back honestly on how and why the decision was made.

[6:30 to 8:30 | 2 minutes] Almost everyone says they make decisions based on data. Taken literally, that is a trap. If only what we have data on enters the decision, then everything we lack data on becomes irrelevant by default, and those are often the factors that matter most. So my approach has two halves. First, build the data we need. When I saw we were guessing about where our students land, I started collecting employment outcomes so the next decision would not be a guess. That data has already shown us something we did not know: a number of our finance students have been placed at Lockheed Martin in Syracuse, a couple of them in supply chain roles. That is a relationship and a career path we can now build on deliberately. Second, ground decisions in the data we have while reasoning openly about what we have to infer. CPA-120 is the live example. We have no data yet on how the new pathway will change graduate accounting enrollment, but curriculum decisions cannot wait for it. So we state our assumptions, decide, and watch the numbers as they arrive. What we cannot measure still counts. It just has to be argued for out loud.

[8:30 to 10:15 | 1 minute 45 seconds] One part of my management style is worth stating directly. I understand management as prioritizing faculty work under a fixed constraint: time. Adding tasks is easy, and it is not management. Anyone can send another request. So I hold myself to a simple rule. If I add a task, I take one away. New work comes with a trade, not on top of everything else. The CPA-120 redesign I mentioned means a lot of forms through the curriculum committee for our accounting faculty. So I went to the university and got them taken off a university-wide service committee to make room for it. The other half is continuous streamlining. I look constantly for processes that cost faculty time without adding value. For example, our faculty were reporting the same publications, teaching, and service materials four separate times: to the dean, to the provost, to our AACSB committees, and for Middle States. I reduced that to a single report that serves all four, so colleagues are not asked for the same information again and again. Time is the resource faculty need most for teaching and research, and it is the one a head can most easily waste.

[13:45 to 15:30 | 1 minute 45 seconds] This is what I mean by accountability. When results fall short, the head owns what happens next. The head does not need to have the answer, but does need to make sure the evidence leads to action. Our general business knowledge measure was the ETS Major Field Test in the capstone, with 145 out of 200 as the benchmark. In spring 2022, 68 percent met it and the mean was 151, about the 37th percentile nationally. We did not stop at the headline. Subscores showed quantitative and economics performance near the 38th percentile while every other category was above average. We took that diagnosis to the full faculty retreat on May 10, 2022. Faculty agreed to short quantitative bell-ringers, practice tools for students who needed them, and explicit review of fundamentals across courses. On the same measure in fall 2023 and spring 2024, 80 percent met the benchmark. Twelve points, the highest in our series. My point is not that the head had the answer. My responsibility was to make sure we looked honestly, acted collectively, and measured again.

[27:30 to 29:30 | 2 minutes] The third opportunity comes from a proposal I submitted to my own college, called AI in Our DNA. Its premise is that AI is not a passing trend, and that the usual response, adding a new AI course next to each existing one, is the expensive way to get there. It doubles the number of courses, needs new faculty or adjuncts, and spreads students across more classes. The alternative is to transform existing courses in place so they become AI-native. Introduction to Business becomes AI in Business. Management Information Systems becomes Cloud and AI Systems. Financial Markets and Institutions becomes Digital Finance: Markets and Institutions, covering tokenization, decentralized finance, algorithmic trading, and digital payments. The proposal called that last one Funding FinTech Startups; I have since broadened it, because FinTech alone is too narrow. Same AI coverage, roughly half the resources, and no new faculty lines. At SUNY Poly, that last point is what made this matter. We have to be careful about adding courses and programs because of faculty sufficiency: every new course has to be staffed by qualified faculty, and under AACSB that pool can only stretch so far before the ratios suffer. Transforming in place gives students AI throughout the curriculum without spreading the faculty thinner. Any AACSB school, including this one, faces the same constraint. You will notice the same principle I described earlier: faculty time is a fixed budget, and our CPA-120 change needed no new courses. Change by transformation, not expansion. Here, the question would be which courses the faculty think are ready, and the faculty who teach them would decide what the transformed version looks like. That faculty-capacity question leads to another already on every AACSB school's calendar: how teaching effectiveness will enter faculty qualification.

[29:30 to 31:00 | 1 minute 30 seconds] This is not primarily an AI question. It is a faculty-governance and faculty-development question that every AACSB school now has to address. Under the 2026 standards, effective this July, Standard 3.2 requires faculty with teaching responsibilities to meet both the engagement requirements for SA, PA, SP, or IP and the school's own teaching-effectiveness criteria. Faculty who do not meet both requirements are classified as Additional. Additional faculty count in the denominator but not the numerator, so they pull down both the 40 percent SA ratio and the 90 percent combined ratio. Schools have a three-year ramp-up, with formal evaluation beginning with 2029-30 visits. That makes the transition period the time to define the criteria, not 2029. I would want that work done with faculty. The criteria should fit the school's mission and instructional roles, draw on multiple forms of evidence rather than a single metric, and put expectations, feedback, and support in writing before the criteria affect anyone's qualification. This is an opportunity to get the system right together, not simply a compliance exercise.

In crypto, I have built a large dataset of tokenized stocks and perpetual futures across multiple decentralized and centralized exchanges. That allows cross-venue research on stock-based crypto assets, which has just become an important area: on September 17 the SEC issued its Innovation Exemption, providing temporary, conditional relief for certain tokenized NMS stocks traded through permissioned Tokenized Securities Venues. Questions about pricing, liquidity, and fragmentation across those venues are now live, and this data is built to answer them. In energy, I write Python and R wrappers for the U.S. Energy Information Administration's API; the R package, EIAdata, is on CRAN. In banking, I built a database of financial data for every FDIC-insured institution, merged with FFIEC institution data. These resources serve more than my own papers. Students use all three in coursework and projects, and the crypto data has been particularly useful for student AI projects. They are also exactly the kind of data access I would want to open to colleagues looking for coauthors and research questions.
