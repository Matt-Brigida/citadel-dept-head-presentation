[2:30 to 4:00 | 1 minute 30 seconds] Shared governance is not a brake on leadership. It is how decisions become durable. Two examples are underway in my department right now. First, the accounting faculty are reworking our undergraduate curriculum for the CPA-120 pathway. The design belongs to them; they own the expertise and they will teach it. My job is to frame the constraints honestly, put the evidence on the table, and carry their conclusion forward with real advocacy. Second, the faculty are deciding to retire a concentration in our Master's in Accounting. Making that decision workable is my part: I am working with our junior faculty to find alternative online courses, so the students are served and those colleagues can keep their time for research. There is a corollary. Personnel decisions and allocations of scarce resources sometimes belong to the head. I would name that boundary rather than stage a consultation whose outcome is already fixed. Faculty can tell the difference.

[5:30 to 7:00 | 1 minute 30 seconds] Almost everyone says they make decisions based on data. Taken literally, that is a trap. If only what we have data on enters the decision, then everything we lack data on becomes irrelevant by default, and those are often the factors that matter most. So my approach has two halves. First, build the data we need. When I saw we were guessing about where our students land, I started collecting employment outcomes so the next decision would not be a guess. That data has already shown us something we did not know: a number of our finance students have been placed at Lockheed Martin in Syracuse, a couple of them in supply chain roles. That is a relationship and a career path we can now build on deliberately. Second, ground decisions in the data we have while reasoning openly about what we have to infer. CPA-120 is the live example. We have no data yet on how the new pathway will change graduate accounting enrollment, but curriculum decisions cannot wait for it. So we state our assumptions, decide, and watch the numbers as they arrive. What we cannot measure still counts. It just has to be argued for out loud.

[7:00 to 8:30 | 1 minute 30 seconds] One part of my management style is worth stating directly. I understand management as prioritizing faculty work under a fixed constraint: time. Adding tasks is easy, and it is not management. Anyone can send another request. So I hold myself to a simple rule. If I add a task, I take one away. New work comes with a trade, not on top of everything else. The other half is continuous streamlining. I look constantly for processes that cost faculty time without adding value. For example, I reduced repeated reporting requests, so colleagues were not asked for the same information again and again. Time is the resource faculty need most for teaching and research, and it is the one a head can most easily waste.

[10:00 to 11:30 | 1 minute 30 seconds] This is what accountability looks like in practice. Our general business knowledge measure was the ETS Major Field Test in the capstone, with 145 out of 200 as the benchmark. In spring 2022, 68 percent met it and the mean was 151, about the 37th percentile nationally. We did not stop at the headline. Subscores showed quantitative and economics performance near the 38th percentile while every other category was above average. We took that diagnosis to the full faculty retreat on May 10, 2022. Faculty agreed to short quantitative bell-ringers, practice tools for students who needed them, and explicit review of fundamentals across courses. On the same measure in fall 2023 and spring 2024, 80 percent met the benchmark. Twelve points, the highest in our series. My point is not that the head had the answer. My responsibility was to make sure we looked honestly, acted collectively, and measured again.

[15:30 to 17:30 | 2 minutes] Several Baker School faculty have already invested in teaching with generative AI. I would not arrive to explain AI. The leadership question is how to give that work shared curricular and assessment infrastructure. If a learning objective is assessed through a memo or take-home analysis, we may no longer know whose competency we measured. That points toward a mixed assessment portfolio: more in-class performance, short oral defenses, and objectives that ask students to direct, check, and defend AI-supported work. There is also a timely accreditation question. My current understanding of the 2026 AACSB standards is that teaching effectiveness moves into Standard 3.2 on the faculty qualification axis. Faculty with teaching duties who do not meet school-defined criteria become Additional and do not count in either the 40 percent SA or 90 percent combined sufficiency ratios. With a three-year ramp-up and first formal evaluation for 2029-30 visits, the transition is when criteria should be developed, not 2029. Advisory board and employer partners could contribute authentic cases and serve as occasional audiences for oral defense, while faculty retain control of assessment design. Verify every standards detail before delivery. Frame this as an opportunity to define credible evidence together, not as a compliance alarm.
